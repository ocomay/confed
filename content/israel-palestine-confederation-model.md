# Confederation Model (Israel-Palestine)

**Summary**: A proposed "third way" political structure for resolving the Israeli-Palestinian conflict — two independent, sovereign states sharing a layer of joint institutions, open borders, and freedom of movement, rather than full territorial separation or full unification.

**Sources**: `ארץ חדשה -האם יש סיכוי לקונפדרציה של שני העמים_transcript.md` (interview with Oren Yiftachel and Rula Hardal, [[a-land-for-all]], Jan 2025) — needs verification against additional sources; currently based on a single advocacy interview (source: transcript).

**Last updated**: 2026-09-02

---

## The Core Idea

The confederation model, as presented by [[a-land-for-all|"A Land for All"]], is framed explicitly as a **third paradigm**, rejecting both of the two dominant historical approaches to resolving the conflict:

1. **Separation** ("division"/הפרדה) — the classic two-state solution as usually conceived: two closed, walled-off states with a hard border. Associated in the interview with Oslo-era diplomacy and unilateral moves like the 2005 Gaza disengagement.
2. **Unification** ("union"/איחוד) — a single state encompassing all of historical Palestine/Israel, an idea the speakers say some Palestinians advocate for.

Both, the speakers argue, have **failed** — separation in particular, after roughly 70 years of attempts, is characterized as having "led to disaster" (source: transcript, 1:13–1:34; this is the speakers' own assessment, not an independently sourced conclusion — needs verification).

### What Confederation Means Structurally

Per the interview (2:04–2:33), the proposed structure has:
- **Two separate sovereign states** — separate Israeli and Palestinian law, separate parliaments
- **A shared institutional layer** — joint mechanisms starting with less politically fraught domains: environment, economy, and (eventually) security
- **Freedom of movement** between the two states over time, intended to "neutralize" the conflict by allowing normal contact between populations

The precedents cited by Yiftachel for this model (10:25, 13:39–14:36) are Ireland/Northern Ireland, Bosnia, the European Union, and (historically) Canada — polities where deeply divided, intermixed populations with a history of violence built confederative or quasi-confederative arrangements. See [[comparative-conflict-resolutions]] for detail on these precedents.

## Rationale Given for the Model

### Shared homeland claim
Both Israelis/Jews and Palestinians, per Yiftachel, relate to **the entire territory** (not a sub-region) as their homeland — a legacy of the British Mandate-era boundaries. Since both populations feel this whole-territory attachment, a model that lets each side retain sovereignty *and* access/connection to the whole land is presented as more durable than hard partition (transcript, 2:59–3:44).

### Palestinian statehood as necessary for Israel's survival
Yiftachel states explicitly that "Israel will not remain [viable/legitimate] without Palestinian sovereignty also existing" (3:44) — i.e., framing Palestinian statehood not as a concession to Palestinians but as a precondition for Israel's own long-term security/legitimacy. This is a normative claim from the speaker, not a factual one.

## Specific Proposed Elements ("Practical Utopia") — as described in the interview

Yiftachel describes several concrete mechanisms (10:46–11:48), which he explicitly labels a "practical utopia" (אוטופיה מעשית):

- **Refugees**: The right of return would formally belong to the **Palestinian state**, not Israel — but freedom of movement would let a person whose family was displaced (e.g., from Acre or the Galilee) visit and work in their family's original location without formal return/resettlement in Israel itself.
- **Jewish residents in Palestinian territory**: Reframes the presence of Jewish settlers — under this model, Jews could live in the Palestinian state, but **under Palestinian law**, not as an extraterritorial or annexing population. Yiftachel explicitly contrasts this with current settlers, whom he calls colonial occupiers (see flagged claim in [[eretz-lekulam-confederation-interview]]).
- **Jerusalem**: Proposed as a **unified but shared city**, explicitly compared to Brussels (a city serving multiple national/linguistic communities) — not physically divided, but not exclusively sovereign to one side either.

## ⚠️ Reconciliation With A Land for All's Actual Published Platform

The interview's description above is a **simplified, personal summary**, not the organization's authoritative platform text. Having now ingested the fuller platform detail (via Wikipedia, see [[a-land-for-all]]), several points need correction or refinement:

- **Refugees**: The actual platform states Palestinian refugees **could return to Palestine, and would gradually have an *option* to reside in Israel** — this is a real (if gradual/limited) pathway to Israel itself, which the interview's "visit and work, not resettlement" framing does not convey. This is a **more expansive** provision than the interview suggests.
- **Settlers**: The actual mechanism is that settlers become **Israeli residents of Palestine** (retaining Israeli citizenship, voting for local Palestinian authorities only) — not simply "living under Palestinian law" as citizens/subjects in the way the interview's phrasing might imply. The platform also explicitly commits to **freezing settlement expansion** and phasing in Israeli residency gradually "to avoid overwhelming the Palestinian state" — an acknowledgment of the demographic-overwhelm risk that doesn't come through in the interview.
- **Jerusalem**: The actual platform describes Jerusalem as **managed by international authorities and religious representatives** — a materially different governance model than the interview's "Brussels" comparison (a national capital shared administratively between two national communities, without heavy third-party/religious administration). **This discrepancy is unresolved** — it's unclear whether Yiftachel's Brussels framing is his own gloss on the same underlying idea, an outdated/superseded platform detail, or a genuine inconsistency in how the organization presents itself across different spokespeople and time periods. Needs verification against A Land for All's own primary materials (alandforall.org).
- **Territorial baseline**: The platform specifies **pre-1967 Green Line borders** as the starting point for the two states — not mentioned at all in the interview.
- **Citizenship mechanics**: Arab Israelis would hold **dual citizenship** of both states; Palestinians in Israel vote in Palestinian elections, Israelis in Palestine vote in Israeli elections — none of this granular mechanic is covered in the interview.
- **Domestic equal-rights commitments**: The platform includes specific commitments to Palestinian citizens of Israel (anti-discrimination law reform, Bedouin recognition in the Negev) that are entirely absent from the interview's discussion, which focuses on the international/statehood dimension rather than domestic Israeli civil-rights reform.

**Takeaway**: Treat this page's "as described in the interview" section as **one spokesperson's compressed summary**, not the definitive platform. See [[a-land-for-all]] for the fuller, Wikipedia-sourced platform detail, which should be treated as more authoritative pending direct access to A Land for All's own primary materials.

## Objections Addressed in the Interview

### "Where's the trust going to come from?"
Host Ruth Klein raises this directly (12:13–12:43). Hardal's response (14:36–17:00):
- Trust-building **cannot wait** for pre-existing trust — trust needs the right *conditions*, not just time.
- References the early Oslo period (early 1990s) as a precedent where hope/openness grew quickly once a process began.
- References the pre-First-Intifada period (mid-1970s to mid-1990s) as one with freer movement and more "normal" contact between the populations — cited as evidence conditions can shift.

### "Won't terrorism just continue?"
Yiftachel's response (12:43–13:39): terrorism, in his account, doesn't vanish overnight anywhere in the world, but subsides when there is a political resolution — citing the Basques, the IRA-era Irish situation, and the Lebanese civil war's end (Taif Agreement) as cases where insurgent violence receded once a settlement was reached. **This is a generalized comparative claim** — needs verification against conflict-resolution literature on a case-by-case basis; presented in the interview without detailed evidence.

## International Legal Framing

Yiftachel positions the confederation proposal as consistent with international law, citing a July 2024 ICJ advisory opinion characterizing Israel's presence in the occupied territories as illegal (18:17–18:40). See flagged claim #4 in [[eretz-lekulam-confederation-interview]] — the ICJ opinion itself needs to be checked directly rather than relied on via this secondhand characterization.

## Balancing Perspectives

This page describes the model as **presented by its advocates**. For critical assessment, see:
- [[confederation-model-critique]] — Palestinian-rights critique (model doesn't go far enough) and realist/security critique (model asks too much); notably, both traditions converge on flagging the ~30x economic/power asymmetry between the two populations as a risk the model doesn't resolve
- [[israeli-palestinian-public-opinion-polling]] — **(revised)** specific confederation-support percentages previously cited here were found to be unverifiable against primary sources and have been retracted; what the confirmed RAND data shows is deep mutual distrust and low confidence in any alternative to the status quo (not confederation specifically) — a real but more modest empirical challenge than the retracted figures implied
- [[israeli-right-wing-opposition]] — the dominant Israeli right-wing position rejects the premise of Palestinian sovereignty entirely, regardless of confederative safeguards; commands comparable or greater public support (~24-29%) than the confederation model itself
