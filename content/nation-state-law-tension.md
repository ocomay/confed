# The Confederation Model vs. Israel's Jewish Nation-State Character

**Summary**: The core legal/ideological tension between the confederation model and Israel's Basic Law defining it as the nation-state of the Jewish people (2018), including the Law of Return dimension — and how different sources address (or fail to fully resolve) this tension.

## The Legal Basis of the Tension

**Basic Law: Israel as the Nation-State of the Jewish People** (passed by the Knesset, July 19, 2018, 62–55) states, in relevant part (primary text, per Wikisource):

- **Section 1.B**: "The State of Israel is the national home of the Jewish people, in which it fulfills its natural, cultural, religious and historical right to self-determination."
- **Section 1.C**: **"The right to exercise national self-determination in the State of Israel is unique to the Jewish people."** — This is the operative clause creating the tension: self-determination in Israel is defined as an **exclusively Jewish** right, not a right belonging to "all the people of a given territory," as self-determination is more commonly framed in international law contexts.
- **Section 5**: "The state will be open for Jewish immigration and the ingathering of exiles" — this is the constitutional/Basic Law-level anchor for the (separately legislated) **Law of Return**, which grants Jews and their descendants worldwide an automatic right to Israeli citizenship.
- **Section 11**: Amendment requires a Basic Law passed by a **Knesset majority** — i.e., this is entrenched at the highest tier of Israel's quasi-constitutional legal order, not an ordinary statute easily reversed.

**The core tension for confederation proposals**: A model built on "two states, shared institutions, freedom of movement, mutual respect for both peoples' self-determination" runs directly into a Basic Law that defines Jewish self-determination in Israel as **unique/exclusive** — not equally shared with, or symmetrically mirrored for, the Palestinian population living in or moving through Israeli territory.

## How Confederation Proponents Address This

### A Land for All's own answer: separate national character + separate immigration law, linked by shared institutions

**Now directly sourced from the organization's own 2014 founding text** (see [[a-land-for-all-primary-platform]] for full quotes): 

> "Both countries will have the right to determine the nature of immigration into them, and their citizenship laws. Therefore, Palestine will be free to grant citizenship to Palestinian refugees, and the State of Israel will be free to grant citizenship to diaspora Jews, **as they see fit**."

This is the **precise operative mechanism**: it doesn't just "preserve" the Law of Return in vague terms — it explicitly reaffirms Israel's **full sovereign discretion** over its own citizenship law, symmetrically mirrored by the Palestinian state's equivalent discretion. Neither state is *obligated* by the confederation structure to extend citizenship to the other's population. This directly answers the Nation-State Law tension **structurally**: Section 1.C's exclusivity claim operates *within* Israel's own citizenship/immigration domain, which the confederation platform explicitly leaves untouched and at Israel's sole discretion.

**Important refinement**: the "gradual option to reside in Israel" for Palestinian refugees (noted in [[a-land-for-all]] via Wikipedia) refers to **residency**, not **citizenship** — the 2014 text is explicit that citizenship remains "as they see fit" for each state. This distinction (residency vs. citizenship) is the load-bearing legal mechanism throughout the whole platform, not just for refugees but also for settlers (who become **residents**, not citizens, of the other state — see [[a-land-for-all-primary-platform]]).

Separately, notably, the 2014 platform's own self-determination language is **more neutral than Israel's Basic Law**: "two sovereign states will exist... where the two people will realize **their** right of self-determination" — plural, symmetrical, and **not** using "Jewish state" terminology explicitly. The platform doesn't contradict Israel's Basic Law, but it also doesn't invoke or affirm the "unique to the Jewish people" framing — it simply operates one level up, at the level of *interstate* citizenship discretion, without engaging the *intrastate* self-determination-exclusivity question at all. The two national self-determination projects run in **parallel, not merged** — the confederation's shared layer is explicitly limited to functional cooperation (water, economy, security), not a merger of national character or citizenship law.

**This is the core proposed resolution**: confederation does **not** ask Israel to relinquish its Jewish nation-state character or the Law of Return — it proposes to *preserve* both national projects side by side, while adding cooperative infrastructure on top. In this framing, the Nation-State Law's "unique to the Jewish people" clause remains legally undisturbed *within Israel's own sovereign territory* — the confederation doesn't claim Palestinians would share in Israeli national self-determination, only that they'd gain their own, symmetrical self-determination in a separate Palestinian state, plus freedom of movement/residency (short of citizenship) across the border.

### "Two States Plus" article (K. Revue, 2025) — a sympathetic source that honestly flags the tension as unresolved
This source (found via search, ingested via WebFetch summary — **full text not yet directly ingested, needs verification**) explicitly identifies the Law of Return as a **"pragmatic realist" objection requiring careful attention** — notably, framed as a real problem to be reckoned with, not dismissed. Key points from this source:

- Acknowledges the Law of Return allows Jews and their descendants to obtain Israeli citizenship and settle in the Jewish state — a right that has no Palestinian equivalent *within Israel* under the confederation model as usually proposed.
- **Identifies the actual destabilizing risk**: under freedom-of-movement provisions, Palestinians could become **long-term non-citizen residents of Israel** (paralleling how the [[a-land-for-all|A Land for All]] platform describes settlers becoming "Israeli residents of Palestine" — the mirror-image arrangement). The article asks pointedly: over generations, would such populations "remain non-citizen residents... without demands for reconnection between the two statuses arising?" — i.e., **could a non-citizen-resident status be stable over time, or would it generate pressure toward full citizenship/political rights**, which would in turn pressure the demographic logic underlying the Nation-State Law?
- **Does not claim to fully resolve this.** The article's own conclusion defers the answer to "confederation's institutional capacity to counterbalance the implications of residential strategies deployed over time" — explicitly citing the European Union as an inspiration for how such tensions might be managed institutionally over time, without claiming Europe's experience directly transfers.

**This is a notably more intellectually honest treatment than the [[eretz-lekulam-confederation-interview|original interview]]**, which did not engage with the Nation-State Law or Law of Return tension at all.

### Dahlia Scheindlin's position (needs primary verification)
Scheindlin — an Israeli pollster/political analyst and confederation advocate whose podcast appearance originally prompted Chacar's 2019 critique (see [[confederation-model-critique]]) — is reported (via search summary, not yet directly verified against a primary interview) to argue that **Israel cannot fulfill its "democratic promise" without committing to equality for all non-Jewish citizens and recognizing Palestinian national self-determination** — a position that appears to be in **tension with, if not direct opposition to**, the Nation-State Law's "unique to the Jewish people" language, though the search summary does not indicate she calls for repealing the law specifically. **Needs verification** against a primary Scheindlin source before treating this as a settled position.

## The Palestinian-Rights Critique of This Resolution (Chacar)

As already documented in [[confederation-model-critique]], Chacar's 2019 critique targets exactly this mechanism — her argument, restated in this context:

By preserving **separate immigration regimes** (Israel's Law of Return for Jews worldwide vs. a Palestinian state's more limited/gradual return provisions for Palestinian refugees — see [[a-land-for-all]] for the "gradual option to reside in Israel" detail), the confederation model **formally ratifies rather than resolves** the core asymmetry Chacar identifies: unconditional national-return rights for one people (Jews, anywhere in the world) vs. conditional/limited/state-mediated return rights for the other (Palestinians, even those with direct ancestral ties to specific locations now inside Israel). Her conclusion — that "Zionism and full civic equality are irreconcilable" — is a direct rejection of the "preserve both national projects in parallel" resolution that A Land for All and the K. Revue article propose. She holds that as long as one state's foundational law defines self-determination as **exclusive to one ethnonational group** (i.e., exactly what the Nation-State Law's Section 1.C does), no amount of confederative cooperation resolves the underlying inequality — it can only manage it.

## Summary of Positions

| Source | Position on the tension |
|---|---|
| **Basic Law (2018), Sec. 1.C** | Self-determination in Israel is legally, constitutionally **exclusive to the Jewish people** — the tension's legal root |
| **A Land for All platform** | Preserves both national projects (incl. Law of Return) **in parallel**, adds cooperative layer on top; does not ask Israel to amend/repeal the Nation-State Law |
| **"Two States Plus" (K. Revue, pro-confederation)** | Honestly flags the tension as a real, only **partially resolved** problem — defers full resolution to future institutional design |
| **Chacar (+972, Palestinian-rights critique)** | Argues the "parallel preservation" approach **cannot** achieve true civic equality; the exclusivity itself (not just its practical effects) is the problem |
| **Scheindlin (confederation advocate, per secondary summary)** | Appears to argue Israeli democracy requires recognizing Palestinian self-determination too — **tension with Sec. 1.C not explicitly reconciled in sources reviewed; needs primary verification** |
