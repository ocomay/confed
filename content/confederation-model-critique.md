# Critique of the Confederation Model

**Summary**: Substantive criticism of the Israeli-Palestinian confederation proposal (as advanced by [[a-land-for-all]] and similar initiatives), from both a Palestinian-rights/progressive angle and a realist/security angle — balancing the advocacy framing presented in [[eretz-lekulam-confederation-interview]].

**Sources** (full text now ingested in `raw/`):
- Henriette Chacar, "Confederation can't answer the most important issue in Israel-Palestine," +972 Magazine, July 2, 2019 — [link](https://www.972mag.com/palestinian-rights-zionism-confederation/)
- Edo Konrad, "Is Israeli-Palestinian confederation all it's hyped up to be?", +972 Magazine, May 3, 2021 — [link](https://www.972mag.com/confederation-israel-palestine-obstacles/)
- RAND Corporation press release, "Israelis Unwilling to Risk Two-State Solution; Palestinians Only Interested in Variant That Israelis Cannot Accept," Feb 10, 2021 — [link](https://www.rand.org/news/press/2021/02/10.html)

**Last updated**: 2026-09-02

## Two Distinct Critical Traditions

Criticism of the confederation model comes from (at least) two directions that don't always agree with each other:

1. **Palestinian-rights / progressive critique** (Chacar): The model doesn't go far enough — it leaves core injustices (Zionism's exclusionary structure, settlement normalization, curtailed refugee return) intact under a cooperative-sounding label.
2. **Realist / security critique** (Konrad; RAND): The model asks too much — it requires levels of trust, open borders, and shared institutions that neither side's security establishment or public is remotely ready for.

## Important Correction: What the RAND Study Actually Found

**The RAND study (Feb 2021) is not primarily about confederation.** Its headline finding, per its own title, is that **"Israelis [are] Unwilling to Risk Two-State Solution; Palestinians [are] Only Interested in Variant That Israelis Cannot Accept."** The study assessed **five** alternatives — two-state solution, one-state solution, confederation, Israeli annexation of Area C, and continuation of the status quo — not confederation specifically.

**Key actual findings** (from the full press release, now ingested):
- **~60% of Israelis** said the status quo could feasibly continue as it has for decades, and considered it preferable to the risk of any alternative — peace was called a "romantic notion."
- Palestinians indicated willingness to accept a **modified two-state solution** — with geographic contiguity, political autonomy, a standing army, and border control — but researchers note this "would be very difficult for Israel to accept."
- **Methodology**: 273 individuals (West Bank Palestinians, Gazan Palestinians, Israeli Jews, Israeli Arabs), four-hour structured focus-group discussions, conducted **2018–2019**, using RAND's Delphi method. (Note: an earlier version of this wiki cited "33 focus groups" from a secondary search-result summary — this figure could not be verified in the primary press release and should be treated as unconfirmed pending the full report.)
- **Funding**: Sponsored by a private gift from Peter and Carol Richards.
- **Co-author Shira Efron** is a senior research fellow at Israel's own **INSS** (Institute for National Security Studies) — i.e., there is a direct institutional link between this RAND study and the INSS polling cited separately in [[israeli-palestinian-public-opinion-polling]].
- The study's own **general takeaway on confederation specifically** is captured secondhand via Konrad's 2021 article (below): "relatively low rates of acceptance... coupled with high rates of uncertainty" — but the primary press release itself does not give confederation-specific percentages. **This page and [[israeli-palestinian-public-opinion-polling]] should not cite specific confederation-support numbers as coming from this RAND study** — they don't appear in the source ingested.

## Palestinian-Rights / Progressive Critique (Chacar, +972 Magazine, 2019)

**Context**: Chacar's article is a direct response to a +972 Podcast episode in which Dr. Dahlia Scheindlin presented Israeli-Palestinian confederation as an alternative to the two-state solution. Chacar engages both with Scheindlin's argument and, by name, with the "Land for All" initiative's own published principles.

### 1. Doesn't confront Zionism's exclusionary structure
Chacar's central argument, quoting the Land for All initiative's own principles directly: Palestinian citizens of Israel would be granted "national minority rights, civil equality and appropriate representation in government institutions, and a fair distribution of national resources." She argues this **cannot achieve true civil equality**, because the model leaves it up to each state to "determine the nature of immigration into them" — preserving Jewish national supremacy (unlimited Jewish immigration rights worldwide via the Law of Return) alongside a much more limited Palestinian claim.

**Contrast with source interview**: Yiftachel and Hardal present "both peoples relate to the whole land as homeland" as a *reason for* confederation. Chacar's critique suggests this framing obscures that only one side's attachment is backed by an enforceable, unconditional immigration/citizenship right (Israeli Law of Return vs. the Palestinian state's more limited, gradually-implemented return provisions — see [[a-land-for-all]] for the actual, more nuanced platform detail on this point).

### 2. Normalizes settlements
Chacar's exact framing: the model "shields Israel from legal accountability by normalizing Israel's settlement project in the occupied West Bank and East Jerusalem." She directly quotes the Land for All initiative: **"The countries will determine an agreed number of citizens to live in their territory and receive permanent residency."** This is an important precise detail — **residency under the plan is number-limited/negotiated, not unconditional free movement** — which she argues nonetheless "equates a settler community that has stolen Palestinian-owned lands with the native populations that were conquered and subjected to Zionist rule."

**Contrast with source interview and actual platform**: Yiftachel frames settlers living "under Palestinian law" as ending (not normalizing) the colonial relationship. The actual platform (per [[a-land-for-all]], sourced from Wikipedia) clarifies settlers would become **Israeli residents of Palestine** (not Palestinian citizens) and the plan explicitly calls for **freezing settlement expansion** — a detail that partially addresses (though does not resolve) Chacar's "rewards annexationist policies" objection.

### 3. Refugee return is curtailed, not honored
Chacar: "the confederation model addresses the Palestinian right of return by limiting those refugees who are allowed to return to being citizens of a future Palestinian state" — and notes an editorial correction to her own article: an earlier version stated refugees could only *resettle* in a future Palestinian state; this was corrected to say they could only *become citizens* of a Palestinian state (a narrower, more precise claim). Combined with the settlement-normalization critique, she argues this "denies refugee, diaspora, and internally displaced Palestinians the right to return to their original homes" and thereby "emboldens Zionism's exclusive claim to this land."

**Cross-reference**: [[a-land-for-all]]'s fuller platform detail (per Wikipedia) shows Palestinian refugees would "gradually have an option to reside in Israel" — a provision **more expansive than either the interview or Chacar's 2019 critique reflects** (her critique predates or doesn't capture this specific gradual-residency provision). **This is a genuine open question for the wiki**: is Chacar's critique addressing an earlier/different version of the platform, or is the "gradual option to reside" provision itself contested/aspirational rather than concrete? Needs verification against primary A Land for All materials directly.

### 4. Obscures power asymmetry
Chacar objects to "both sides would have to make concessions" framing as concealing Israel's overwhelming military, economic, and political dominance — arguing instead for "a strategy that recognizes the stark asymmetry in power and exerts pressure on Israel to relinquish its claw of occupation." This is echoed, from a different angle, in the realist critique below.

### Chacar's Underlying Framework
Her conclusion is explicit and goes beyond critiquing confederation specifically: "It is imperative that we shift from a peace-making system obsessed with resuming a distorted negotiation process, to a rights-based approach that aspires toward decolonization, and accepts that Zionism and full civic equality are irreconcilable." **This is a maximalist position** — it implies no framework preserving a Jewish-majority state alongside full civic equality (as opposed to a single democratic state) can satisfy her standard. Readers should note this as the article's actual normative conclusion, not merely a critique of confederation's specific mechanics.

## Realist / Security Critique (Konrad, +972 Magazine, 2021)

**Context**: Konrad's article responds to J Street (a prominent pro-two-state US lobby group) spotlighting confederation proposals at its 2021 annual conference — noting confederation's growing appeal even within the traditional two-state camp. Konrad's own framing: confederation is "an attempt to reconcile the contradictory impulses that inform support for both two-state and democratic one-state proposals," but "does not necessarily make it the best option."

### 1. Low awareness and acceptance
Citing the RAND 2021 study specifically: "relatively low rates of acceptance of this formula coupled with high rates of uncertainty" among Israeli and Palestinian focus-group participants. Unlike the two-state solution, which — whatever one's view of its viability — is broadly *understood* as a concept by both populations, confederation remains largely confined to "academic circles" and Western policy discourse.

### 2. Security architecture conflicts with Israeli threat perception

**⚠️ Disambiguation (corrected 2026-09-02, per user query)**: The source text raises **two distinct border issues** that this page previously conflated. Precise breakdown:

**(a) The core issue — the Israel-Palestine border itself.** Konrad's central point: "For a country consumed by concerns over physical safety, confederation, with its porous borders, seems like a poor fit." Even under a two-state outcome, Israel would want to "retain firm control over what and who comes in and out of the country" — i.e., firm control at the Israel-Palestine crossing points specifically. The **Egypt/Jordan comparison is used only as an analogy** for Israel's general security disposition ("this is in keeping with the manner in which Israel protects its frontiers with Egypt and Jordan, decades after signing peace treaties") — it illustrates that Israel maintains tight border control **even with countries it's formally at peace with**, not a claim that confederation would somehow open those specific frontiers.

**(b) A separate, more ambitious feature of some confederation proposals — shared control of Israel's own external frontiers.** Direct quote, referring to a distinct point: "Given Israeli reluctance to even accept an American or international force in the West Bank under previous peace plans, it seems highly unlikely that it would share control over its external borders with the Palestinians, **as some confederation proposals recommend**." This is about certain confederation models going further than open Israel-Palestine movement — proposing that Palestinians get a formal role in jointly managing Israel's international frontiers (with Egypt, Jordan, airports, etc.), not just the internal Israel-Palestine crossing. Konrad flags this specific, more far-reaching feature as especially unlikely to be accepted by Israel — distinct from (and beyond) the baseline open-border-between-the-two-states concept.

**Net effect on the critique**: The security objection is strongest regarding (a) — Israel's control over its own entry points from Palestinian territory — which is the core, unavoidable feature of any confederation model. Objection (b) applies only to the more expansive versions of confederation that propose joint management of Israel's other international borders, which is not a universal feature of all confederation proposals (and is not mentioned in [[a-land-for-all]]'s platform as documented so far — that platform describes each state controlling entry at its own discretion, including "the right to deny entry to hostile individuals," rather than jointly-managed external frontiers with third countries).

### 3. Economic power imbalance (~30x income gap)
Sourced to the authors' own **2020 Israel Policy Forum study**, "In Search of a Viable Option": the roughly **thirty-fold** income disparity between Israelis and Palestinians could make Palestine "the junior partner" economically under confederation. Direct comparison: East Germany's economic decline and population exodus after 1990 reunification with West Germany — where the income gap was **only a few thousand dollars**, dramatically smaller than the Israeli-Palestinian gap. A Palestinian respondent from East Jerusalem, quoted via the RAND study: **"Under the confederacy Israel will keep all its power. We will continue being the weak side."**

**This is a striking convergence** with the Palestinian-rights critique above (#4) — from a different angle (material/economic rather than ideological), both critiques land on the same concern: **confederation risks freezing in place the existing power imbalance rather than resolving it.**

### 4. Settler presence as a governance/safety problem
Keeping "Israeli settlers inside an independent Palestine — many of whom are ideologically opposed to Palestinian statehood, and some who have pursued violence to this end" is flagged as a practical governance and safety risk for Palestinians specifically — a different angle on the same structural feature Chacar criticizes as "normalization" (her framing is about legitimizing occupation; Konrad's is about physical safety and sovereignty for Palestinians living alongside a hostile settler population).

### 5. Institutional mismatch
Direct quote: Israel's system "is considerably more democratic than the Palestinian Authority's single-party dictatorship." Konrad argues that absent "regime change that unseats Fatah" and resolution of the West Bank-Gaza political split, it's hard to see how a democratic and non-democratic government could function together in a shared supranational framework — a friction point absent from Israel's prior state-to-state peace treaties (Egypt, Jordan), which didn't require shared governing institutions.

### Historical precedent Konrad notes (not in the interview)
UN Resolution 181 of 1947 — the original partition plan — **already included a proposed economic union** between the Jewish and Arab states envisioned at that time. Joint institutions are not a new idea in this conflict's history; Konrad frames confederation as reviving, not inventing, this approach.

### Konrad's Own Conclusion
Notably more measured than Chacar's: some proponents see confederation as a way to "reenergize the drive toward two states" rather than as a wholly distinct paradigm. Konrad's closing assessment: "it is fair to critique the present viability of two states, as well as more utopian one-state designs. But confederation, in many respects, is even more ambitious than both of these ideas, and absent more clear-eyed assessment, it risks failing along the same standard." **This is a "grant the critique of the alternatives, but note confederation isn't exempt from it either" framing** — distinct from Chacar's more categorical rejection.

## Where the Critiques Converge

Despite starting from opposite political premises (one arguing confederation is too soft on Israel, the other that it's unrealistic to expect Israeli security concessions), **both critical traditions independently flag the same core problem**: the ~30x economic/power asymmetry between the two populations, and the risk that a "shared institutions" framework simply formalizes Israeli dominance rather than equalizing it.
